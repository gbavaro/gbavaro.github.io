A Big Audience Is Not a Business

What happens when new technology or behavior becomes commercially important.

Guy Bavaro October 12, 2026

A product can reach hundreds of millions, even billions, of people and still not have a repeatable commercial model.

Scale creates opportunity. It does not tell you what a customer should buy, what outcome they should expect, what the product should cost, or how the company needs to operate to deliver it.

That part has to be built.

I’ve worked through versions of this at Microsoft, Snap, X/xAI and now EA. The technology changes. The behavior changes. But many of the commercialization questions stay remarkably consistent.

One lesson keeps coming back:

The commercial challenge is not simply finding demand. It is building the system that turns demand into adoption.

And the first question is still the simplest one:

Why are people using this in the first place?

The behavior comes first

New technology usually becomes commercially interesting because it first becomes useful enough to change behavior.

At Microsoft, search was changing how people found information and made decisions.

At Snap, visual communication was changing how people communicated.

At X, real-time information and conversation were changing how people experienced sports, culture and news.

At EA, the behavior is different again.

Fandom matters, but commercially I think participation is even more interesting.

People aren’t just watching sports or entertainment. They’re playing, competing, building and spending meaningful time inside experiences they care about.

And in many cases, they’ve paid for the game itself or chosen to spend money inside it.

That changes the value exchange.

Traditional media largely monetizes attention. Gaming can monetize participation.

The player has chosen the experience. They may have paid to enter it, invested time in it, built identity around it or continued spending inside it.

That creates a different responsibility for the commercial model.

You’re not simply placing a message next to content.

You’re entering an experience the player chose and invested in.

The commercial experience has to earn its place.

AI is creating another behavior shift.

The behavior is moving from search to conversation, and increasingly from conversation to action.

People are asking questions, creating, planning, comparing options, working through decisions and increasingly asking AI to complete tasks on their behalf.

That is commercially interesting for a different reason.

Search became incredibly valuable because it could capture intent after a user expressed it.

AI may increasingly sit inside the process of forming the intent, evaluating the options and eventually taking the action.

That creates enormous commercial potential.

It also raises the bar for trust, utility and relevance.

The first question still shouldn’t be:

Where can we put advertising?

It should be:

Why are people here, and can a brand or commercial partner add something useful without taking away from the reason they came?

Sometimes the answer may be no.

Advertising is the commercial lens I know best, but I don’t think every new technology needs an advertising model.

The utility has to come first.

Sometimes demand isn’t the problem

I learned this pretty clearly at Snap.

Advertisers were interested in augmented reality.

But interest didn’t automatically turn into adoption.

The friction was everything around the product.

Creative production could be complicated. Advertisers were learning something new. Agencies needed new capabilities. Education, approvals and internal processes had to evolve.

So the better question wasn’t:

How do we sell more AR?

It was:

What’s stopping adoption?

We worked with a holding company and teams inside Snap to rethink parts of the process across creative, strategy, education, approvals and production.

The lesson wasn’t really about AR.

A commercial problem isn’t always a demand problem. Sometimes the operating model is getting in the way.

That distinction has stayed with me.

I saw another version of it at X.

You could have executive interest and a clear reason for the platform to matter, but that didn’t automatically turn into advertiser adoption.

The people actually making media decisions still had to understand the product.

Was it easy enough to buy?

Did they know where it belonged in the plan?

Could we prove an outcome?

Did the teams responsible for execution know how to activate it?

The question wasn’t:

How do we get advertisers to spend more?

It was:

What is stopping a buyer who sees the value from acting on it?

That is a go-to-market question as much as it is a sales question.

Your first customers should teach you something

Companies can optimize for revenue too quickly when they’re building something new.

Revenue matters. Obviously.

But early on, what you learn from the first customers can be more valuable than maximizing the first deal.

I tend to think of those early customers as development partners.

Did we drive an outcome?

What did they value?

What didn’t work?

What couldn’t we measure?

What made adoption harder than it needed to be?

What would make them do it again?

And most important:

What did we learn that should change the product?

Those answers tell you what the product needs to become, what it may be worth and how it should be priced.

An advertiser may test something because it’s new.

They scale it because it works.

You can’t build this inside Sales

I’ve spent most of my career building and leading commercial teams, which is why I feel strongly about this:

Sales cannot build a scaled commercial business by itself.

The commercial organization brings the market into the company.

We hear what customers are asking for, where demand exists and where things are getting stuck.

Then the work moves across the organization.

Product and Engineering determine what can be built.

Data and Measurement help determine whether it works.

Legal and Privacy make sure the model can operate.

Finance understands the economics.

Operations has to deliver it repeatedly.

Strategic partnerships can help accelerate what the company shouldn’t build alone.

Collaboration doesn’t mean saying yes to everything.

Sometimes the product isn’t ready.

Sometimes the measurement isn’t there.

Sometimes the economics don’t work.

I don’t think the commercialization leader is supposed to have every answer.

I certainly don’t.

The job is to ask the right questions, get the right people together and connect what the market needs with what the company can actually build.

The first deal isn’t the business

A contract isn’t the business either.

Executive alignment matters. It opens doors.

But someone closer to the customer eventually has to believe in the product enough to recommend it.

The advertiser has to understand why it matters.

The product has to work.

The outcome has to be measurable.

And the organization has to be able to deliver it again.

That is the part I find most interesting.

What did the customer teach us?

What stopped adoption?

What needs to change?

Can we prove the outcome?

How should we price it?

Can the go-to-market model support it?

And then:

Can we do it again?

That is the real test.

A large audience gets attention.

A successful product creates utility.

But a business requires something harder: an outcome customers will pay for, a model the organization can deliver and a system that works again and again.

That is the real work of commercialization.
