A Big Audience Is Not a Business

What happens when new technology or behavior becomes commercially important.

By Gaetano “Guy” Bavaro · October 11, 2026

A technology company can have millions of users and still not have a repeatable commercial model.

A big audience creates an opportunity. It doesn’t tell you what a customer should buy, what outcome they should expect, what the product should cost, or how the company needs to operate to deliver it.

That part has to be built.

I’ve worked through versions of this at Microsoft, Snap, X/xAI and now EA. The technologies and behaviors have changed, but I keep asking many of the same commercialization questions.

Why are people using this in the first place?

The behavior comes first

New technology usually becomes commercially interesting because it first becomes useful enough to change behavior.

At Microsoft, search was changing how people found information and made decisions. At Snap, visual communication was changing how people communicated. At X, real-time information and conversation were changing how people experienced sports, culture and news.

At EA, the behavior is different again.

Fandom matters, but commercially I think participation is even more interesting. People aren’t just watching sports or entertainment. They’re playing, competing, building and spending meaningful time inside experiences they care about.

AI is especially interesting because the utility is developing before many commercial models are settled. Users are already changing how they find information, create, solve problems and make decisions.

That is usually when I become interested in the commercialization opportunity.

The first question shouldn’t be, “Where can we put advertising?” It should be:

Why are people here, and can a brand or commercial partner add something useful without taking away from the reason they came?

Sometimes the answer may be no. Advertising is the commercial lens I know best, but I don’t think every new technology needs an advertising model.

The utility has to come first.

Sometimes demand isn’t the problem

I learned this pretty clearly at Snap.

Augmented reality was something advertisers were interested in. But interest didn’t automatically turn into adoption. Creative production could be complicated. Advertisers were learning something new. Agencies needed new capabilities.

Instead of asking, “How do we sell more AR?” the better question became:

What’s stopping adoption?

We worked with a holding company and teams inside Snap to rethink parts of the process across creative, strategy, education, approvals and production.

The lesson wasn’t really about AR:

A commercial problem isn’t always a demand problem. Sometimes the operating model is getting in the way.

I saw another version of that at X.

You could have executive interest and a clear reason for the platform to matter, but that didn’t automatically turn into advertiser adoption.

You still had to understand the people making media decisions. Did they understand the product? Was it easy enough to buy? Did they know where it belonged in the plan? Could we prove an outcome?

The question wasn’t, “How do we get advertisers to spend more?” It was:

What is stopping a buyer who sees the value from acting on it?

That is a go-to-market question as much as a sales question.

Your first customers should teach you something

Companies can optimize for revenue too quickly when they’re building something new.

Revenue matters. Obviously.

But early on, what you learn from the first customers can be more valuable than maximizing the first deal.

I tend to think of those early customers as development partners.

Did we drive an outcome? What did they value? What didn’t work? What couldn’t we measure? What would make them do it again?

Most important:

What did we learn that should change the product?

Those answers tell you what the product needs to become, what it may be worth and how it should be priced.

An advertiser may test something because it’s new. They scale it because it works.

You can’t build this inside Sales

I’ve spent most of my career building and leading commercial teams, which is why I feel strongly about this:

Sales cannot build a scaled commercial business by itself.

The commercial organization brings the market into the company. We hear what customers are asking for, where demand exists and where things are getting stuck.

Then the work moves across the organization.

Product and Engineering determine what can be built. Data and Measurement help determine whether it works. Legal and Privacy make sure the model can operate. Finance understands the economics. Operations has to deliver it repeatedly. Strategic partnerships can help accelerate what the company shouldn’t build alone.

Collaboration doesn’t mean saying yes to everything. Sometimes the product isn’t ready, the measurement isn’t there or the economics don’t work.

I don’t think the commercialization leader is supposed to have every answer. I certainly don’t.

The job is to ask the right questions, get the right people together and connect what the market needs with what the company can actually build.

The first deal isn’t the business

A contract isn’t the business either.

Executive alignment matters. It opens doors. But someone closer to the customer eventually has to believe in the product enough to recommend it. The advertiser has to understand why it matters. The product has to work. The outcome has to be measurable.

The first deal matters. I’m more interested in what happens after it.

What did the customer teach us? What stopped adoption? What needs to change? Can we prove the outcome? How should we price it? Can the go-to-market model support it?

Can we do it again?

That’s what makes technology commercialization interesting to me.

Whether it’s AI, gaming or whatever comes next, technology can change behavior much faster than companies can figure out the business model around it.

AI is particularly interesting because the utility is already becoming part of how people work, discover, create and make decisions while commercial models are still developing.

What do users value here? Where can a commercial partner add value? What outcome could that create? And can the company build a repeatable model around it without damaging the trust or utility that brought the user there?

I’ve seen the technology change many times.

The commercialization questions are remarkably consistent.

And when the outcome becomes repeatable—and the organization can repeatedly deliver it—that’s when you stop having an interesting product or audience.

You start having a business.
