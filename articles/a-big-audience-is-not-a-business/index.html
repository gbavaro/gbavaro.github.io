A Big Audience Is Not a Business

What I’ve learned about turning new user behavior and new technology into repeatable commercial adoption.

Guy Bavaro October 12, 2026

I’ve spent a lot of my career working at the point where a new technology or new user behavior starts becoming commercially important.

The technology changes, but the questions I keep coming back to usually don’t.

What is the user behavior telling us?

What’s stopping commercial adoption?

What outcome are we actually creating?

What needs to change to make it easier to buy, use and scale?

And eventually:

Can we do it again?

I’ve seen different versions of those questions across search, social, real-time media, gaming and now AI.

The pattern is pretty consistent.

A big audience is not a business.

Interest is not adoption.

And the first deal is not the model.

What is the user behavior telling us?

I think commercialization starts with behavior.

Before thinking about what to sell, you have to understand why people are using the product in the first place and what is changing in the way they behave.

Search changed how people found information and made decisions.

Social changed how people communicated, discovered things and expressed themselves.

Real-time platforms changed how people experienced sports, culture and news.

Gaming changed something else.

People aren’t simply watching. They’re playing, competing, building and participating.

That distinction matters.

Traditional media largely monetizes attention. Gaming can monetize participation.

The user has chosen the experience. They may have paid for it. They’ve invested time in it. In many cases, they’ve built identity around it.

That changes the commercial opportunity, but it also changes the responsibility.

The commercial experience has to earn its place.

AI is creating another shift in user behavior.

People are moving from search to conversation, and increasingly from conversation to action.

They’re asking questions, comparing options, planning, creating and working through decisions.

Search became incredibly valuable because it captured intent once someone expressed it.

AI may increasingly sit inside the process where that intent is actually formed.

That creates a very different commercial opportunity.

But I still think the first question should be:

What is the user behavior telling us, and can a commercial experience add value without getting in the way of it?

Sometimes the answer may be no.

That’s okay.

Utility has to come first.

What’s stopping commercial adoption?

This is probably the question I ask most often.

I learned it pretty clearly at Snap.

Advertisers were interested in augmented reality.

Interest wasn’t necessarily the problem.

Commercial adoption was.

Creative production could be complicated. Advertisers were learning something new. Agencies needed new capabilities. Education, approvals and internal processes had to evolve.

So the better question wasn’t:

How do we sell more AR?

It was:

What’s stopping someone who already sees the value from actually adopting it?

We worked across teams and with a holding company to rethink parts of the process around creative, education, strategy, approvals and production.

That experience stayed with me because the lesson was much bigger than AR.

A commercial problem isn’t always a demand problem. Sometimes the operating model is getting in the way of adoption.

I saw another version of that at X.

The challenge wasn’t always interest in the platform. A lot of the friction came from brand safety and perception.

Advertisers could see the value of real-time conversation and culture, but they also had questions about whether the environment was right for their brand.

So the question wasn’t simply:

How do we get advertisers to spend more?

It was:

What is stopping a brand that sees the value from feeling comfortable enough to adopt it commercially?

That meant understanding the concerns, separating perception from reality where we could, improving the areas we could control, and giving advertisers the confidence to make a decision.

Again, the issue wasn’t always demand.

Sometimes the barrier to commercial adoption is trust.

What outcome are we actually creating?

A customer may test something because it’s new.

They scale it because it works.

That sounds obvious, but I think it gets lost when companies get excited about early demand.

Early customers are important for revenue.

But they’re also important because they teach you what the product actually needs to become.

What did they value?

What didn’t work?

What couldn’t we measure?

What made adoption harder?

What would make them do it again?

And probably the most important question:

What did we learn that should change the product?

Those answers help tell you what the product is worth, how it should be priced and what the go-to-market model needs to look like.

That’s why I tend to think of early customers as development partners.

The first deal should teach you something.

What needs to change?

This is also why I don’t think commercialization can live entirely inside Sales.

Sales brings the market into the company.

We hear what customers are asking for. We see where demand exists. And we usually see pretty quickly where commercial adoption is getting stuck.

Then the work becomes cross-functional.

Product and Engineering determine what can actually be built.

Data and Measurement determine whether we can prove it worked.

Legal and Privacy determine whether the model can operate.

Finance helps determine whether the economics make sense.

Operations has to figure out how to deliver it repeatedly.

Partnerships can help accelerate things the company shouldn’t necessarily build itself.

I don’t think the commercialization leader is supposed to have every answer.

The job is to ask the right questions, get the right people together and connect what user behavior and the market are telling us with what the company can actually build.

That’s why I increasingly think about commercialization less as a sales problem and more as a system-building problem.

Can we do it again?

This is where the difference between a deal and a business becomes pretty clear.

The first customer matters.

The first contract matters.

Executive alignment matters.

But none of those things, by themselves, create a scalable business.

The user behavior has to be real.

The customer has to understand the value.

The product has to work.

The outcome has to be measurable.

The economics have to make sense.

And the company has to be able to deliver it again.

So I keep coming back to the same questions:

What is the user behavior telling us?

What stopped commercial adoption?

What did the customer teach us?

What needs to change?

Can we prove the outcome?

Can we scale the go-to-market model?

And finally:

Can we do it again?

Because that is the real test.

A large audience gets attention.

Strong user behavior creates opportunity.

A first deal creates momentum.

But repeatable commercial adoption is what turns all of that into a business.

That is the real work of commercialization.
